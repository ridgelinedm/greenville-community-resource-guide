# Greenville Community Resource Hub
## Project Master Document

**Working title:** Greenville Community Resource Hub  
**Document purpose:** Single source of truth for the project's goals, UX direction, design principles, prototype scope, and next steps.  
**Status:** Working project brief / handoff  
**Audience:** Project team, designers, developers, stakeholders, and future collaborators

---

## 1. Project Overview

The Greenville Community Resource Hub is intended to help people in Greenville find community resources and, more importantly, understand **what to do next** when they need help.

The project should not be treated simply as a resource-directory redesign. The existing resource information is a foundation. The larger opportunity is to create a much clearer, more humane experience on top of that information.

The central design challenge is:

> **Can someone open this website and understand what to do next within five seconds—without anyone explaining the site to them?**

The site should make it easier for people to move from a difficult situation to a practical next step.

---

## 2. The Core UX Idea

### Don't make it feel like a directory. Make it feel like a guide.

A traditional directory asks the visitor to know what organization, category, or service they are looking for.

A guide starts with the person's situation and helps them determine a useful next step.

The experience should therefore prioritize:

1. **What is happening to me?**
2. **What do I need right now?**
3. **What can I do next?**

The interface should reduce the amount of interpretation required from the visitor.

---

## 3. The Grandmother Test

A key project principle is the **Grandmother Test**:

> **Could a person who is stressed, unfamiliar with the local resource system, or not especially comfortable with technology open the site and understand what to do next within five seconds?**

This is not literally a test of a grandmother. It is a reminder to design for people who may be:

- stressed or frightened;
- embarrassed or overwhelmed;
- unfamiliar with social-service terminology;
- using a phone;
- searching late at night;
- helping another family member;
- dealing with an urgent or confusing situation;
- unsure which organization or government agency they need.

If the site requires the visitor to understand the organizational structure before receiving help, the experience is probably too complicated.

---

## 4. Primary Audience and Human Context

The project has a particularly important responsibility toward people dealing with incarceration, re-entry, and the effects of incarceration on families.

Relevant users may include:

- A family member trying to understand what happens after someone is arrested.
- A spouse or partner trying to find immediate practical help.
- A parent or grandparent trying to support someone who is incarcerated.
- Someone preparing to return to the community after incarceration.
- Someone who has recently been released and needs housing, food, employment, transportation, identification, treatment, or other support.
- Someone helping another person navigate these systems.

These visitors should not have to know the name of a program before they can find it.

---

## 5. Signature User Journeys

The project should give special attention to two signature journeys.

### Journey A: Family / Loved One Experiencing Incarceration

The visitor may arrive with questions such as:

- What happens now?
- How do I find out where someone is?
- How do I communicate with them?
- What can I bring or send?
- What happens in court?
- What should I do about children, housing, money, or other immediate concerns?
- Where can I get help?

The site should translate these questions into understandable next steps rather than sending the person into a generic directory.

### Journey B: Re-entry / Returning to the Community

A person returning from incarceration may need several kinds of support at once.

Potential needs include:

- housing;
- food;
- employment;
- transportation;
- identification and documents;
- healthcare;
- behavioral-health or substance-use support;
- benefits;
- legal assistance;
- family support;
- other immediate stabilization needs.

The interface should help the visitor prioritize what to do first rather than presenting a long, undifferentiated list of organizations.

---

## 6. Homepage Direction

The homepage should immediately communicate:

1. **You are in the right place.**
2. **We can help you figure out what to do next.**
3. **You do not need to know the name of an organization.**

The homepage should be organized around situations and needs rather than around the internal structure of the resource database.

### Suggested hierarchy

#### A. Clear welcome / orientation

Use plain language to explain what the Hub does.

The visitor should understand the purpose without reading a long introduction.

#### B. Start with your situation

Provide prominent pathways based on what the visitor is experiencing.

Examples of possible entry points:

- I need help right now
- Someone I love is incarcerated
- I am getting ready to come home
- I need housing
- I need food
- I need a job
- I need transportation
- I need healthcare
- I need legal help

These are examples, not a final taxonomy.

#### C. Immediate / urgent help

Make urgent needs easy to identify without making the entire site feel like an emergency-services page.

#### D. Search / browse as a secondary path

A conventional search or resource directory can remain available for visitors who know exactly what they want.

The important distinction is that **search should not be the only way to begin**.

---

## 7. Action Cards

A major interface concept is the **action card**.

Instead of simply presenting an organization and a description, a card should help answer:

> **What can this resource do for me, and what should I do next?**

A useful card may include:

- What this helps with
- Who it is for
- What to expect
- Location / service area
- Hours
- Phone / contact method
- Website, when relevant
- Whether an appointment is required
- Cost, when known
- Accessibility information, when known
- A clear next action

The card should make the next step visually and verbally obvious.

### Example structure

**Need help finding a place to stay?**

**What they help with:** Emergency and transitional housing support.

**Good to know:** Call first to check availability.

**Next step:** `Call for availability`

The exact content should come from verified resource information.

---

## 8. Language Principles

The site should use **plain, direct, respectful language**.

Prefer:

- "Find a place to stay"
- "Get help with food"
- "Someone I love is incarcerated"
- "Get ready to come home"
- "Find a job"
- "Get legal help"

Avoid unnecessarily bureaucratic language such as:

- "Housing stabilization resources"
- "Justice-involved populations"
- "Beneficiary eligibility pathways"

unless such terminology is required for accuracy and is explained in plain language.

### General writing rule

Write for the person who needs help, not for the organization providing it.

---

## 9. Tone

The experience should feel:

- welcoming;
- calm;
- respectful;
- practical;
- trustworthy;
- nonjudgmental;
- human.

It should **not** feel:

- bureaucratic;
- clinical;
- institutional;
- patronizing;
- overly cheerful in situations where people may be hurting;
- like a government form;
- like a marketing funnel.

The site should communicate dignity without becoming sentimental.

---

## 10. Accessibility

Accessibility is a core requirement, not a later enhancement.

The experience should be designed for:

- mobile phones;
- people with visual impairments;
- people with limited digital literacy;
- people under stress;
- people who may have cognitive or attention difficulties;
- people using assistive technologies;
- people with limited bandwidth or older devices, where practical.

Important considerations include:

- readable typography;
- strong contrast;
- clear focus states;
- large, understandable touch targets;
- semantic structure;
- descriptive link and button labels;
- accessible forms;
- keyboard navigation;
- screen-reader-friendly content;
- avoiding information conveyed by color alone;
- clear error messages;
- predictable navigation.

---

## 11. Mobile-First Thinking

Many visitors will likely encounter the Hub on a phone.

The mobile experience should therefore be treated as a primary experience rather than a compressed desktop design.

Important priorities:

- The most important actions should appear early.
- Buttons should be easy to tap.
- Phone numbers should be easy to call.
- Addresses should be easy to use for navigation.
- Content should be scannable.
- Long descriptions should not bury the next action.
- Important information should not depend on hover.
- The interface should work well under stressful, distracted conditions.

---

## 12. Trust and Accuracy

A resource hub has a special responsibility to avoid sending someone toward outdated or incorrect information.

Resource information should therefore distinguish between:

- verified information;
- information that may change;
- information that still needs confirmation.

Where appropriate, the interface should communicate practical caveats such as:

- "Call first to confirm availability."
- "Hours may change."
- "An appointment may be required."

The project should avoid presenting uncertain information with false confidence.

---

## 13. The Existing Resource Database

The existing resource database should be treated as an important foundation.

The redesign does **not** need to begin by rebuilding the entire underlying resource ecosystem.

The first objective is to improve the experience of discovering and acting on resources.

The interface should provide a useful layer over the existing information.

This allows the project to validate the UX direction before investing heavily in a complete rebuild.

---

## 14. Prototype Scope

The recommended initial prototype is intentionally small.

Rather than attempting to design the entire website at once, start with three screens:

### Screen 1 — Homepage

Purpose:

- Establish the guide-oriented experience.
- Test whether visitors immediately understand what the site is for.
- Test the "five-second" orientation principle.
- Establish the primary situation-based pathways.

### Screen 2 — Family / Incarceration Journey

Purpose:

- Demonstrate how the site handles a complicated and emotionally difficult situation.
- Organize information around practical questions and next steps.
- Test whether the visitor can move from "I don't know what to do" to a useful action.

### Screen 3 — Resource Recommendation / Action Card

Purpose:

- Demonstrate how an individual resource is presented.
- Make the next action obvious.
- Test whether the card provides enough information without becoming overwhelming.

This three-screen prototype should be treated as a learning tool, not as the final information architecture.

---

## 15. What Success Looks Like

A successful experience should allow a visitor to:

1. Understand what the Hub is for quickly.
2. Recognize their situation or need.
3. Choose a relevant path without knowing an organization's name.
4. Understand what the recommended resource does.
5. Know what to do next.
6. Take that action with minimal friction.

The experience should reduce the feeling of:

> "There are hundreds of resources here. Which one am I supposed to call?"

and replace it with:

> "Okay. This looks like the next thing I should do."

---

## 16. Design Questions to Keep Asking

Throughout the project, evaluate decisions against questions such as:

### Orientation
- Can someone understand the homepage in five seconds?
- Is the purpose obvious without explanation?

### Relevance
- Can someone start with their situation instead of an organization name?
- Does the interface reflect how people actually describe their problems?

### Action
- Does every major recommendation make the next step clear?
- Can the visitor easily call, visit, apply, or otherwise act?

### Cognitive load
- Are we asking the visitor to make too many decisions?
- Can information be simplified without becoming inaccurate?

### Human dignity
- Does the language respect people who are incarcerated, returning from incarceration, or supporting someone who is?
- Does the design avoid making people feel judged?

### Accessibility
- Can a person using a phone navigate it comfortably?
- Can someone using assistive technology understand the structure?
- Are actions obvious without relying on color or visual cues?

---

## 17. What Not to Do

Avoid turning the redesign into:

### A giant list

A huge list of resources is not automatically useful.

### An organization-first experience

Do not force visitors to know which organization or agency they need before they can receive guidance.

### A maze of categories

Too many categories can simply move the burden of figuring things out from one page to another.

### A polished directory with the same underlying UX

A visual redesign alone does not solve the core problem.

### A generic "community resources" website

The experience should feel grounded in the actual situations people in the community are facing.

### A complete rebuild before testing the concept

The project should validate the guide-oriented model with a focused prototype before committing to the entire system.

---

## 18. Open Decisions

The following items should be decided through research, stakeholder input, content review, and prototype testing rather than assumed.

### Information architecture

- What are the final top-level situations?
- Which needs deserve direct homepage pathways?
- Which categories should remain available through search/browse?

### Resource data

- What fields already exist in the resource database?
- Which fields are reliable?
- Which fields are missing?
- How is resource freshness maintained?

### Incarceration / re-entry content

- What specific questions should the family journey answer?
- What specific sequence best supports someone preparing for release or recently released?
- Which information requires legal or agency verification?

### Emergency information

- Which situations should trigger an urgent-help pathway?
- How should emergency services be distinguished from ordinary community resources?

### Branding

- What existing visual identity should be retained?
- What visual language communicates trust without feeling institutional?

### Technology

- What platform currently powers the resource database?
- What technology is available for the new interface?
- How should the guide experience connect to the underlying data?

---

## 19. Research and Testing

The project should be tested with people who resemble the intended users.

Testing should focus on tasks rather than asking whether participants "like" the design.

Example tasks:

- "You just found out someone you love has been incarcerated. Show me what you would do first."
- "You have been released and need somewhere to stay. Show me where you would start."
- "You need food today. What would you click?"
- "You've found this organization. What would you do next?"

Observe:

- where people hesitate;
- what they misunderstand;
- which labels they expect;
- whether they can identify the next action;
- whether they trust the information;
- whether they can complete the task without assistance.

---

## 20. Proposed Development Sequence

### Phase 1 — Understand

- Review the existing resource database.
- Identify available data fields.
- Review existing site structure.
- Identify the highest-value user journeys.
- Confirm stakeholder priorities.

### Phase 2 — Prototype

Build the three-screen prototype:

1. Homepage
2. Family / incarceration journey
3. Resource action card

### Phase 3 — Test

Test the prototype with representative users.

Focus on:

- five-second comprehension;
- situation recognition;
- navigation;
- action clarity;
- accessibility;
- emotional tone.

### Phase 4 — Refine

Use testing results to revise:

- navigation;
- labels;
- page hierarchy;
- cards;
- language;
- content priorities.

### Phase 5 — Expand

Only after the core model proves useful should the project expand into the broader resource ecosystem.

## 22. The North Star

The project should continually return to one question:

> **Can she open this website and understand what to do next within five seconds—without anyone explaining the site to her?**

If the answer is yes, the project is moving in the right direction.

If the answer is no, more features, more categories, and more visual polish are unlikely to solve the underlying problem.

---

The best immediate next step is to create and review the three-screen prototype:

1. **Homepage**
2. **Family / incarceration journey**

The prototype should be evaluated primarily as a **wayfinding and decision-making experience**, not as a finished visual design.

> **Don't make it feel like a directory. Make it feel like a guide.**
